Your ship

← → put the rudder over. ↑ ↓ call the oar-stroke. SPACE to begin.

Rowing hard carries you through the danger sooner, but a fast hull answers the rudder slowly — and the gulf pulls a heavy ship just the same.

Twelve oars, twelve men. Watch the benches: every man Scylla takes leaves his oar trailing dead in the water, and you can count what you have left without looking away from the strait.

What Homer says

Scylla has six necks, six heads, three rows of teeth in each, and twelve dangling feet. Her voice is the yelp of a newborn puppy — skylax — which is where her name comes from.

Charybdis swallows and spews three times a day. To be swallowed is death; the spew merely throws you.

Circe's counsel

Hug Scylla's cliff and lose six men — one to each head — rather than lose the whole ship in the gulf. The strait is a bowshot wide: at Homer's settings there is no safe lane between them.

Odysseus survived Charybdis only once, alone, by clinging to a wild fig tree above the vortex until his mast was vomited back up. You can see that fig leaning over the gulf; if she takes your ship, SPACE or a tap is the only control you get.
